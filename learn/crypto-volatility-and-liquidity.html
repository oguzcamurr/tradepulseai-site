Volatility and liquidity in crypto, explained

How far price tends to move, how easily you can trade, and why the combination matters for every order.

TradePulse AI guides · Published 24 October 2026

Volatility and liquidity are two of the most practical things to understand about any crypto market. Volatility tells you how far price tends to move. Liquidity tells you how easily you can trade without moving the price yourself. Together they shape how wide price swings are, how reliable a stop-loss level is, and how much a trade can cost beyond the quoted price.

Volatility: how much price moves

Volatility is the size of price changes over a period, regardless of direction. A coin that moves 5% a day is more volatile than one that moves 1% a day, whether those moves are up or down. Crypto assets are generally far more volatile than major stock indices or currencies, and smaller coins are usually more volatile than bitcoin.

Measuring volatility with standard deviation

The statistical approach takes the percentage change of each candle and calculates the standard deviation of those returns. This is often called realised or historical volatility. It is usually annualised for comparison; because crypto trades every day of the year, crypto volatility is typically annualised over 365 days rather than the roughly 252 trading days used for stocks.

Measuring volatility with ATR

Average True Range (ATR), introduced by J. Welles Wilder Jr. in 1978, measures the typical size of each candle's range, including gaps. For each candle, the true range is the largest of:

  • the current high minus the current low,
  • the current high minus the previous close (absolute value),
  • the current low minus the previous close (absolute value).

ATR is the average of the true range over a period, usually 14 candles. It is expressed in price units, so an ATR of 1,000 on bitcoin and 0.05 on a small coin can't be compared directly. Dividing ATR by the current price (ATR percent) makes different assets comparable.

Why volatility matters

  • Stop distance. A stop-loss placed closer than the normal swing of the market is likely to be hit by noise. Many traders size stops in multiples of ATR for this reason.
  • Position size. The same position size carries more risk in a more volatile market. Leverage multiplies this.
  • Indicator behaviour. High volatility makes oscillators such as RSI swing between extremes more often.

Liquidity: how easily you can trade

Liquidity is the ability to buy or sell an asset quickly without moving its price much. A liquid market has many buyers and sellers, tight prices and plenty of size available near the current price. An illiquid market may show an attractive quoted price that disappears as soon as you try to trade size.

Ways to gauge liquidity

  • Trading volume: how much has traded over a period, often measured in USDT or USD. Higher volume usually, but not always, means better liquidity.
  • Bid-ask spread: the gap between the best price a buyer is offering and the best price a seller is asking. Wider spreads mean a higher cost every time you trade.
  • Order book depth: how much size is waiting at prices near the current one. Shallow books mean larger orders move the price.

Volume and depth are not the same thing. A market can show high volume over a day yet have little size resting in the order book at a given moment.

Slippage

Slippage is the difference between the price you expected and the price you actually got. It grows when liquidity is thin, when markets move fast, and when orders are large relative to the book. Stop-loss orders that trigger market orders are especially exposed: in a fast move through a thin book, the fill can be well beyond the stop level.

How volatility and liquidity interact

The riskiest combination is high volatility with low liquidity: price swings are large and each swing is harder to trade at a fair price. Liquidity also changes over time. It is often thinner during weekends, holidays and off-peak hours, and it can drop sharply during sudden market moves, exactly when many traders want to exit. For leveraged futures, regulators such as the CFTC note that leverage magnifies losses and can lead to positions being closed out quickly; see the sources below.
