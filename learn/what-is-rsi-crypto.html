What is RSI in crypto?

How the Relative Strength Index is calculated, how to read 70, 30 and 50, and why it often misleads in strong crypto trends.

TradePulse AI guides · Published 24 October 2026

The Relative Strength Index (RSI) is one of the most widely shown indicators on crypto charts. It turns recent price changes into a single number between 0 and 100 that describes momentum: how strongly price has been rising or falling over a recent window. This guide explains how RSI is calculated, how the common readings are usually interpreted, and where the indicator tends to mislead, especially in crypto markets.

Where RSI comes from

RSI was developed by J. Welles Wilder Jr. and published in his 1978 book New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems, the same book that introduced Average True Range (ATR) and the Average Directional Index (ADX). Wilder designed it as a momentum oscillator: it does not measure price itself, but the balance between recent gains and recent losses.

How RSI is calculated

RSI uses a lookback period, by default 14 candles. For each candle, the change from the previous close is recorded as either a gain or a loss. The indicator then averages those values:

  1. Average gain: the average of the upward moves over the period (down moves count as zero).
  2. Average loss: the average of the downward moves over the period, expressed as a positive number.
  3. Relative strength (RS): average gain divided by average loss.
  4. RSI: the RS value converted to a 0–100 scale.

RS = average gain / average loss
RSI = 100 − 100 / (1 + RS)

A quick example: if the average gain over the last 14 candles is twice the average loss, RS is 2 and RSI is 100 − 100/3 ≈ 66.7. If gains and losses are equal, RS is 1 and RSI is exactly 50. If there were no losses at all, RSI is 100.

After the first window, Wilder's method smooths the averages: each new average keeps 13/14 of the previous value and adds 1/14 of the newest gain or loss. Some charting tools seed or smooth the averages differently, so two apps can show slightly different RSI values for the same coin and timeframe. Small differences are normal and usually not meaningful.

What "14 periods" means in crypto

The period is counted in candles, not days. On a 1-hour chart, RSI(14) looks at roughly the last 14 hours. On a daily chart it covers 14 days. Because crypto markets trade 24 hours a day, seven days a week, a 14-day crypto RSI covers exactly two calendar weeks, while a 14-day RSI on a stock covers 14 trading days, closer to three calendar weeks. Always check which timeframe an RSI value belongs to before comparing it with another one.

How RSI readings are usually interpreted

Overbought and oversold: 70 and 30

Wilder suggested 70 and 30 as reference levels. Readings above 70 are commonly called "overbought" and readings below 30 "oversold". These labels describe how one-sided recent price action has been. They do not say that a reversal is due. Some traders use 80 and 20 for very volatile assets to reduce the number of extreme readings.

The 50 line

Because RSI is 50 when average gains and losses are equal, many traders treat readings above 50 as momentum leaning up and readings below 50 as momentum leaning down. In practice, RSI often crosses 50 many times in a sideways market, so this works best as context rather than a trigger.

Divergence

A divergence happens when price and RSI disagree: for example, price makes a new high while RSI makes a lower high. Wilder described this as a possible sign of fading momentum. Divergences can persist for a long time, and in a strong trend many of them never lead to a reversal.

Where RSI misleads

  • Strong trends. In a persistent uptrend, RSI can stay above 70 for long stretches while price keeps rising. Reading every overbought value as a sell signal tends to fail exactly when the trend is strongest. The same applies to oversold readings in a downtrend.
  • Short timeframes. On 1-minute to 15-minute charts, RSI swings rapidly between extremes. Many of those swings are noise.
  • Thin markets. For small coins with low trading volume, a single large order can move price enough to push RSI to an extreme. The reading reflects that one move, not broad market behaviour.
  • It only uses closing prices. RSI ignores volume, the size of intraday ranges and anything outside the chart. It is one view of price, not a full picture.

Using RSI as context

RSI is most useful as one piece of context alongside other information. Common practice is to check the broader trend first (see trending vs ranging markets), look at the same indicator on a higher timeframe, and consider volatility and liquidity before reading too much into a single value. No RSI level, on its own, tells you what price will do next.
